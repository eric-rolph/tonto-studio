TONTO STUDIO / FIELD GUIDE

Playing and patching TONTO

Start here

Choose a patch, click Start audio and play the keyboard. A W S E D F T G Y H U J K play notes; Z and X change octaves. Each cabinet has a level fader. The cabinets are monophonic; several cabinets can sound together.

Live signal

The orange trace shows the output after the master level, including tape playback. The green trace shows microphone audio after the preamp. Both use the same time window. Window changes the time scale; Display gain enlarges the traces without changing audio or the peak meters. Dock keeps the display visible while you scroll through the cabinets.

Patch a cable

Click or drag from an output to any green-ringed input. You can begin at either end. One output can feed several inputs. Escape cancels a loose cable. Click a connected input to restore its normal signal. The cable inspector also lets you connect ports by name.

Microphone and MIDI

Enable mic, then use Patch microphone or MIC OUT. Any input accepts the microphone signal. Use MIC ENV to control envelopes or gates with your voice's loudness. Set the destination's gain or modulation amount to hear the result. Use headphones while monitoring. MIDI note, velocity, pitch bend, sustain, CC1/11 expression and CC7 master level are supported.

Modern and historical modes

Modern mode converts pitch scales, trigger polarity and connector types. It preserves continuous pitch bends; it does not quantize. Historical mode keeps raw voltage. Cables with mismatched connectors remain open until you fit an adapter in the inspector. Add a pitch scaler or trigger converter when needed. Bond banana-connected cabinets using their GROUND switches to remove simulated hum. Normal connections include the appropriate internal conversions in both modes.

A 1.2 V/octave source into a 1 V/octave input stretches an octave to 14.4 semitones; the reverse compresses it to 10. The manual scaler also accepts negative gain and a DC offset. Its output is untyped CV, so its voltage is not scaled a second time.

Sequencer and utilities

Load patch 11, 12 or 13 and start audio. The three rows run together: A sets pitch in semitones; B and C output volts for timbre or loudness. Play sends a gate, Rest keeps the voltage but removes the gate, and Skip removes that step. Length and direction change the pattern. Keyboard transposes A lets keys shift its pitch. On small screens, scroll the rows sideways to reach all eight steps.

Patch Row A to an oscillator pitch input and Sequence gate to its envelope trigger. Clock pulse runs through rests; its ÷2, ÷4 and ÷8 outputs can drive slower parts. An external cable to Sequence clock replaces the tempo clock: Start arms it, and each rising edge advances. External gate duration follows the incoming pulse. Reset also resets the divider and switch. Step advances once and can audition a stopped sequence.

The three-way switch normally selects A, B and C, advancing on Clock ÷2. Replace its inputs with audio or other CV. The signed mixer combines three signals; a negative gain inverts one. Both VCAs normally follow the extra rise/fall function, which is triggered by Sequence gate. Patch their signal inputs to hear them. Quantizer normally takes Row A; Slew follows the quantizer. Slew times specify seconds per volt. Connect any utility output to a cabinet input to hear its effect.

The Moog 960, ARP 1601 and Buchla 245 used voltage sequencing; these controls combine useful period functions with modern conveniences, rather than duplicate one historical panel. Noise, sample-and-hold, ring modulation, lag and envelope following are also available in the existing ARP cabinet.

Spectral studio

Try patches 15–18. Filter audio normally receives the Moog voice; fourteen faders shape its low, high and twelve middle bands. The separate multimode filter exposes low-pass, band-pass and high-pass outputs together. Cutoff CV is one volt per octave.

For the vocoder, load Microphone vocoder, enable mic and speak. Play keys to change carrier pitch. The microphone controls twelve envelopes that shape a Moog saw carrier; their meters show the active bands. Patch any audio source into Modulator or Carrier. Sensitivity and Release set the response; Formant shift moves the carrier filter frequencies. Monitor chooses the cabinet output; the individual jacks are always available.

EMS matrix

Rows are sources and columns are destinations. Click a pin to connect; click again to remove it. Choose full, half or inverted strength before placing a pin. Several rows can feed a column. A cable to an EMS input overrides that matrix column. Drag the joystick or use its arrow keys.

Patch memory and tape

Save stores controls, connections, adapter settings, ground bonds, sequence and matrix in this browser. Export makes a portable JSON file. Import also accepts patches from 2600 Studio. Audio stays on your computer. Tape records a stereo cabinet mix and a separate preamplified microphone stem, with eight three-minute takes, varispeed, reverse, layers and WAV export. Recordings are held in memory. Save session downloads the patch, tape settings and full-resolution recordings together. Open session restores that file in this studio. Save before closing or reloading; patch JSON alone does not include audio.

What is modeled

The ARP cabinet runs the existing 2600 Studio DSP with its full patch surface. The other cabinets use original browser DSP: band-limited oscillators and a nonlinear ladder; an FM oscillator, wavefolder and vactrol-style low-pass gate; a three-pole diode-style filter and trapezoid; and macro voices, a delay resonator and overlapping grains.

These are playable approximations, not measured component-level replicas. The Eurorack modules are not Plaits, Clouds or Rings ports. The electrical model uses nominal impedances, pitch standards, trigger thresholds and optional synthetic hum. It does not predict the behavior or safety of physical hardware. Live audio requests 48 kHz; the display reports the audio context rate, and the browser converts to the device when needed. Higher rates remain available for offline reference renders; oscillator oversampling is internal to the Moog and ARP models. Named historical patches describe techniques, not exact recording reconstructions. No manufacturer affiliation.
